## [0:00] Cold open
Three hundred and fifty-two aerial victories. More than any other fighter pilot in history. This is the story of Erich Hartmann: the boy who learned to fly in a glider, the young pilot who broke almost every rule on his first mission, and the prisoner who was not freed for ten years.

## [0:22] 1922–1937 · Weissach, China, gliders
Erich Hartmann was born on the nineteenth of April, nineteen twenty-two, in Weissach, in southern Germany. His father, a doctor, moved the family to China to find work after the First World War. They returned in nineteen twenty-eight, when civil war broke out. His mother, Elisabeth, was one of Germany's first female glider pilots, and she taught him to fly. At fourteen, he was a gliding instructor in the Hitler Youth. He spent a year at a Nazi secondary school in Rottweil, and earned his pilot's licence in nineteen thirty-seven. His younger brother, Alfred, served as a gunner on a Stuka in North Africa. The British captured him. He spent four years as a prisoner of war.

## [1:08] 1940–1942 · Training
He joined the Luftwaffe on the first of October, nineteen forty. In March, nineteen forty-two, he was fined and confined to his room for a week after performing aerobatics, against regulations, during a gunnery flight. That same afternoon, his roommate took the flight Hartmann had been scheduled for. The engine failed. His roommate crashed, and was killed. Hartmann later said the punishment saved his life. He adopted a new credo: fly with your head, not with your muscles.

## [1:42] 1942 · Eastern Front
In October, nineteen forty-two, he joined Fighter Wing fifty-two on the Eastern Front. His first job was ferrying Stuka dive-bombers. His first flight ended when the brakes failed, and the Stuka destroyed the controller's hut. On the fourteenth of October, he flew his first combat mission. Impatient, he broke away from his leader, nearly collided with an enemy fighter, and ran out of fuel. His commander sentenced him to three days with the ground crew. Twenty-two days later, he scored his first victory. By the end of the year, he had only two. Because of his youthful looks, he earned the nickname Bubi: the Kid.

## [2:24] The method
His teacher was his first leader, Paule Roßmann. Hold back. Choose a target that is not taking evasive action. Close in. Hartmann turned it into a motto: See. Decide. Attack. Break. He held his fire until he was twenty metres away, or closer, then fired a short burst at point-blank range, and left. Günther Rall said Hartmann was a very good pilot, but not a virtuoso. His edge was an extremely cool mind. When the British test pilot Eric Brown asked how he did it, Hartmann said: close range, and weak Soviet defensive armament and tactics.

## [3:06] 1943 · Kursk to the Knight's Cross
In July, nineteen forty-three, over Kursk, his score began to climb. On the seventh of July, he scored seven victories in a single day. By late August, he had ninety. On the twentieth of August, debris from an Il-2 forced him to land behind Soviet lines. Soviet soldiers put him on a stretcher, onto a truck. He pretended to be badly injured, escaped, and was back with his unit three days later. On the twentieth of September, he scored his hundredth. On the twenty-ninth of October, he received the Knight's Cross, with a score of one hundred and forty-eight.

## [3:47] 1943–44 · The Black Devil
The Soviets knew his radio call sign: Karaya One. They put a price of ten thousand rubles on his head. They called him the Black Devil, and a black tulip painted on his engine cowling became his mark. The story goes that opponents avoided his aircraft, and his scores dropped, so he had the tulip removed. In reality, he flew with it only five or six times.

## [4:14] 1944 · Romania
In May, nineteen forty-four, he met American Mustangs for the first time, over Romania. Later that June, P-51s ran his Messerschmitt out of fuel. Out of ammunition too, he bailed out. As he hung beneath his parachute, one American pilot, Lieutenant Robert Goebel, flew straight at him. Goebel was making a camera pass, to record the bailout. He banked away at the last moment, and waved.

## [4:43] 1944 · Peak
On the seventeenth of August, nineteen forty-four, he passed Gerhard Barkhorn to become the top-scoring fighter ace of all. A week later, on the twenty-fourth, he scored eleven victories in two missions, taking his total to three hundred and one. The next day, he was awarded the Diamonds to the Knight's Cross, one of only twenty-seven given to German soldiers. At Hitler's headquarters, the Wolf's Lair, he reportedly refused to hand over his pistol. Hitler allegedly told him that, militarily, the war was lost. In September, he married Ursula Paetsch, his sweetheart since his teens. Twice, General Adolf Galland offered him a place flying the Me 262 jet. Twice, he chose to stay with his unit.

## [5:32] 1945 · The end of the war
His last victory came on the eighth of May, nineteen forty-five, over Brno: a Yak-9, hours before Germany surrendered. Soviet forces were already within artillery range of the airfield. His unit destroyed Karaya One, twenty-four other Bf 109s, and its ammunition. Hartmann chose to surrender to the American Ninetieth Infantry Division. On the fourteenth of May, the Americans handed him over to the Soviet Union.

## [6:02] 1945–1955 · Captivity
In Soviet custody, he refused to spy on fellow officers, and was put in solitary confinement for ten days. He went on a hunger strike. In nineteen forty-nine, a Soviet court sentenced him to twenty years. A second trial, in nineteen fifty-one, raised it to twenty-five. He was convicted of war crimes, and refused to confess. His son, Erich-Peter, was born in nineteen forty-five, and died in nineteen forty-eight. Hartmann never saw him. After ten years in prison camps, he was released in late nineteen fifty-five.

## [6:40] 1956–1997 · After
In nineteen fifty-six, he joined the new West German air force, and later commanded Fighter Wing seventy-one, Richthofen. He opposed buying the F-104 Starfighter, over safety concerns. In time, a hundred and sixteen German pilots died in F-104 crashes outside of combat. His criticism was unpopular, and he was forced into retirement in nineteen seventy. Erich Hartmann died on the twentieth of September, nineteen ninety-three. In nineteen ninety-seven, Russia cleared him of all charges.

## [7:16] Legacy
After the war, a biography, The Blond Knight of Germany, made Hartmann famous in America, Britain and Germany. Historians have criticised it. Jens Wehner points to its uncritical borrowing from Nazi propaganda. Ronald Smelser and Edward Davies count it among the books that built the myth of the clean Wehrmacht. The man was real. The myth came later.
